# Rules and auto-reply

Rules (some apps call them filters) are tiny standing instructions: *whenever mail arrives matching this, do that automatically*. Combined with the three folders, rules are how the tidy stays ten minutes instead of an hour.

## Rules worth having (steal these)

Start with any two of these; each takes under a minute to create.

1. **Newsletters to Someday.** If sender contains the newsletter's address, or subject pattern matches, then: skip the inbox, mark as read (optional), file to **Someday**. The inbox only shows mail a human chose to send you.
2. **Receipts and orders to a receipt stash.** Anything with "invoice", "receipt", or "order confirmation" from stores you buy from → skip inbox, file to **Someday** (rename it "Someday and receipts" if that reads better). Tax time, you're a hero: search the folder, not four years of history.
3. **Social network noise** (if you can't quit it outright): known notification senders → skip the inbox. Nothing there is urgent; nothing there is your boss.
4. **The boss/partner/kid rule:** just the opposite — a starred or "Needs action"-pinned rule for the two or three people you never want to bury. Most clients can't auto-violate the tidy order, but keeping those threads findable in one flag or label helps.

### Making one (generic steps)

Gmail: gear → See all settings → **Filters and Blocked Addresses** → Create a new filter → set sender/subject → *Create filter* → choose "Skip the Inbox" and "Apply the label".

Outlook: **Settings → Mail → Rules** → Add new rule, same ingredients (condition, action).

Apple Mail: **Settings → Rules → Add Rule**. Mind the difference: Apple's server-side rules run when Mail is open, so give it a run (or restart) before assuming the rule did its job.

Every client's menu names drift a little; the three-ingredient shape (condition → skip inbox → file) doesn't.

## Rules are "set", still worth a monthly glance

Two reasons. First, one or two rules will always be slightly wrong (the newsletter you actually like lands in the landfill; the invoice from your co-op goes to Social). The weekly reset (chapter 10) includes a one-rule-a-week review, which is enough to catch drift. Second: mail sometimes vanishes and rules are the usual suspect — search the rules *before* concluding anything weirder.

## Auto-reply: the kind version

The vacation responder is not just for vacations — it's for any stretch of time when replies will lag. The good ones share three facts: you're away (or slower) until a date, who to contact instead for urgent stuff, no essay. Draft:

```text
Subject: (auto) Slower replies until __date__

Hi there! I'm away from email until __date__, with limited (or no) access
in the meantime. For anything urgent about __topic__, please
contact __name__ at __contact__ and they'll help faster than I can.
I'll reply when I'm back, even if it takes a couple of days.
Thanks for your patience!
__Name__
```

Notes from someone who has read too many out-of-offices:

- **"Limited access" and a date** do most of the work. People forgive delays; they hate not knowing.
- **Keep it four lines.** No poem, no reasons, no itinerary. "Away" or "limited access" is the only detail strangers need.
- **Turn it on for everyone.** Filtering known contacts only misses the point: the rule calms exactly the people you can't predict.

If someone emails while you're heads-down on a project — same idea, one sentence: *"Heads down on __project__ this week; replies may lag until __date__. For anything urgent, call __who__."* That's an auto-reply *or* a canned response your client's templates feature can insert in two clicks.

## Canned responses

Most clients have some version: Gmail has Templates (enable in Settings → Advanced), Outlook calls it Quick Parts, and there's always the low-tech route of keeping favorite replies in a note. Whatever the mechanism, the use is the same as the paper templates: pre-decided wording for repeated messages, so the only cost is the blanks.

> **If you only do one thing:** create the newsletters-to-Someday rule. It's the one rule that pays for itself every single day, and it's the first domino that makes the ten-minute tidy easy.

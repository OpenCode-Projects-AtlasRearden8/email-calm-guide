# Fast, kind replies

Here's the secret the slow repliers don't know: **kindness doesn't scale with length.** A warm three-sentence reply sends tonight; a "perfect" seven-paragraph reply sends never. The kindest thing you can do for someone is to answer them at all, promptly and plainly.

## The three-sentence default

Most replies need exactly three things: *here's your answer*, *here's the one condition or caveat*, *here's what happens next*. That's it. Try it on the next "just checking in":

> Thanks for the nudge! It's on my list for Friday. If that slips, I'll flag it to you Thursday.

Short replies are not curt. Radio silence is what reads as rude. The person on the other end mostly wants to *know*, not to be dazzled — and a quick, plain answer is a form of respect for their time. You are not being cold; you are being clear, and clear reads as kind once the reply exists.

Long replies still have a place — delicate conversations, real partnerships, apologies with weight behind them. When the stakes are human, go slower. The goal is to stop treating *every* email like it's delicate.

## Reply, wait, or no reply

You're allowed to not reply at all. The cheat sheet:

- **Information only** ("the meeting is at 3"): a "Got it, thanks" costs six seconds and keeps the loop closed. Or archive with love; nobody reasonable audits your acknowledgements.
- **Question aimed at you:** three sentences, now.
- **CC-and-FYI:** archive. No reply was requested; none is owed. (If it turns out something's on fire, the new email will say so.)
- **Group thread you're on:** mute or archive; reply only when you're actually addressed.

## Making a short reply land well

Three tiny habits do the heavy lifting:

1. **Answer the actual question first.** Lead with it, explain after — no throat-clearing ("Sorry for the delay", long weather reports of your week). Context *after* the answer if needed.
2. **Name the next step with a date.** "I'll send it by Thursday" — this is the sentence that both calms them and creates your own accountability.
3. **One thread, one topic.** If you have two separate things to say to the same person, two emails beat one confusing one.

And the boundary lines are worth rehearsing — "I can't make this meeting, but here's what I can do", "I'm not the right person, but X is, and here's their address", "I can't take this on this month". Templates 20, 30, and 40 in the library cover these; stealing them is encouraged.

## Speed tip: reply from the tidy, not from dread

During the ten-minute tidy, send every sub-two-minute reply on the spot. During the rest of the day? Nothing. Email is not chat. The reply window is a decision you already made, so the random 3pm guilt-checks stop being a thing.

> **If you only do one thing:** adopt the three-sentence reply for a week and see if anyone complains. (Almost nobody will; the ones who do are usually asking about the actual content, not the length.)

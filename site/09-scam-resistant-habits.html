Scam-resistant habits

One unkind truth before the tips: fraud works because your inbox keeps you busy and worried, and worried people click fast. A calm inbox is a security upgrade, which is one more quiet reason for the ten-minute tidy. Here are the habits that catch nearly everything, even on low-sleep days.

The three-touch check

For any message that asks you to do something (click, pay, open, confirm), touch these:

  1. Who really sent it? The display name lies freely; open the details and read the actual address. PayPal <paypa1-support@strange-domain.net> is a classic. Also watch official-sounding freemail: the IRS ships from .gov, not a gmail.
  2. Where does the link actually go? Hover (long-press on a phone) and read the real destination under the text. If the words say "shipping update" and the address says something else entirely, believe the address.
  3. Does the story make sense? Did you order anything from there? Does "your subscription" even exist? Would this organization really ask you for this? Scammers rely on you not asking the third question.

Ten seconds. It's the pause that saves everything — the "act now" pressure is the whole product they're selling.

The short red-flag list

Urgency plus unusual payment is the big one: gift cards, wire transfers, crypto, "buy three one-hundred-dollar cards". No real organization works that way. The rest of the consistent tells:

  • Password or code asks. No real service asks you to confirm a password by email, ever.
  • Slight name changes: paypa1, micros0ft, extra hyphens, odd domain extensions.
  • Threats in both directions: "act now" AND "your account will be closed". Legit mail has a customer-service tempo; scams need panic.
  • Unexpected attachments, especially invoices, shipping labels, or "documents" you didn't ask for — and extra sharp eye on .zip, .exe, or any office file that demands you "enable macros".
  • Requests to move the conversation somewhere private ("let's continue on a different app"), away from anyone who might intervene.
  • Business email compromise: "Your email address has changed, please send the payroll here" or a "contractor's" new invoice details from a lookalike address. Ninety-nine percent of email fraud at work is this, so payment-detail changes get verified out of band — always.

Verify out of band — the habit that beats everything

If the message could matter (money, credentials, someone's account, a package), contact the person or company through a channel you already trust: the phone number on the card in your wallet, the app, the office's real number — never the contact details inside the suspicious email itself.

This holds double for small businesses. A supplier asking for updated banking details gets a phone call before a single transfer is re-routed. The two minutes of phone embarrassment costs nothing; a misdirected payment costs an audit.

Family and clinic add-ons

  • Agree on a safe-word with the people most likely to be impersonated: parents, kids, spouse. "EOB" for insurance calls, "the shelter dog" between siblings. If a "please help, this is an emergency" message can't drop the safe-word, it doesn't get your passport money.
  • Trust phone-first instincts. If it "sounds like the hospital", hang up and call the hospital's published number; if it "sounds like the office", call the office the way you normally do.
  • Report instead of regret. "Report phishing"/"Report junk" in your client removes it and improves everyone's filters. Five seconds.

When you clicked something anyway

It happens to sharp people, under pressure, and usually less grave than the fear spike. Close the tab. If you entered a password anywhere, change it on the real site right now (from a different device if you have one), turn on two-factor if it isn't already, and mention it to IT or whoever administers the account. If you entered card details, call the bank back — the number from the card. The faster the report, the smaller the mess; nobody needs to be perfect here, just quick.

If you only do one thing: adopt the three-touch check as a habit for every message that asks for action — ten seconds, every time, catches nearly everything this manual is worried about.

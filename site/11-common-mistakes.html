Common mistakes

Nobody avoids all of these — you'll recognize a few because you've tried them, and that's fine; every one of these is a normal thing tired humans do with email. This page is simply the short trip past each one.

The big table

The mistakeWhat's behind itThe gentler fix
Treating the inbox as the to-do listIt's the fastest single place that already existsThree folders (ch. 2): Needs action is your to-do list, and the inbox stays a landing strip
Reading an email five times without decidingDeciding is the expensive partDecide on first contact: reply, move, or archive. "Read again later" is not one of the options
Forty-seven foldersFiling feels like tidinessThree folders + search. If it needs a second home, it's a rule's job, not yours
Guilt-subscribing ("it's a good cause")The cause stays good; the email still never gets readThree-strikes rule (ch. 4). Support causes directly; unsubscribe from the newsletter
Replying long to prove effortLength feels like sincerityThree sentences + a date. What people remember is the answer, not the word count
Checking email all day "in case"Open loops (ch. 1) make the inbox feel aliveTen-minute window + notifications off. Nothing is on fire that you'd see sooner that way
Snoozing into oblivionSnooze postpones the decision, not the workSnooze = archive + future date you'd actually act. If there's no such date, it's a no
Saving every email forever "just in case"Storage anxietySearch finds things by words, names, dates. Keep nothing you can't explain keeping
Seamlessly skipping the weekly resetIt wasn't anchored in the calendarTreat the reset as a standing appointment (ch. 10); skip weeks freely, keep the habit
Sending an AI draft unreadThe tool is confident and sometimes wrongRead before send; AI drafts are drafts (ch. 7)
Asking someone else's opinion instead of decidingFailing alone is scary; sharing is slowerDecide within the tidy; ask only when the decision is genuinely theirs

The pattern worth noticing

Look down that list: almost every mistake is a version of deciding later. Later-decisions cost more each time — more context lost, more re-reading, more dread compounding. Every chapter of this manual is some flavor of "decide once, cheaply, now".

And the meta-mistake behind the meta-mistake: believing the calm comes from more discipline. Every fix in the table above is smaller than the mistake it replaces. Calm email is small habits and fewer places to look, which is good news if "more discipline" was never in stock.

If you only do one thing: pick the row you flinch at most and do its gentle fix exactly once this week. One row per week quietly beats overhauling everything on Sunday and quitting by Thursday.
